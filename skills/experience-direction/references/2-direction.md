# Stage 2. Direction

How the scenario's course is told: point of view, pace, what is shown and what is hidden, where attention goes.

## Gate

**Requires `scenario.md`. Until `storyboard.md` is written, stage 3 does not begin.**

A storyboard is a list of shots: what is in frame, where we look from, how long it lasts, what replaces it. Not sketches — decisions in words.

## How direction differs from the scenario

The scenario says **what happens**. Direction says **how it is seen**.

The same course can be told ten ways, and they produce ten different things. Choosing the way is the work.

## Four decisions to make

### 1. Point of view

Where the person looks from, and who they are in this world.

    from above       a map reader: everything is surveyable, nothing is frightening
    first person     inside: sees only what is in front of them
    from the side    a spectator: the scene is separate from them
    from inside      they are the mechanism itself

Point of view decides more than any effect. Changing it mid-piece is a strong move, but it must be an event, not carelessness.

### 2. Pace

Where it is slow, where fast, where it pauses. Pace is not animation duration; it is **how much the person manages to understand per unit of motion**.

A rule that is almost always right: **a pause before an appearance works better than a more complex appearance.** One movement instead of ten simultaneous ones.

### 3. What is hidden

Showing everything means showing nothing. Decide explicitly:

- what is visible immediately, before any action;
- what opens only through action;
- what never opens, but is felt to be there.

The third item is what gives a piece depth. A world without edges reads as a set.

### 4. Where attention goes

In every shot, exactly one place holds the eye. If there are two, there are zero.

A trick from theatre: **the light goes out on everything except one thing.** The rest exists but does not compete. This works literally (light) and also through contrast, size, motion, the silence around it.

## The first shot

Separately and strictly. **The first shot is what ends up in the preview, the share card and the first glance.** It must show the concept — not a logo, not a heading.

Test: if the person closes the tab after three seconds, what did they take? "Nothing" means there is no first shot. "A mood, but not whose it is or what is here" means the first shot hides the main visitor's answer — a failure too, however strong the mood.

Hiding (below) is for depth, never for the basics. The name and what is on offer are visible in the first shot, in the concept's own language.

## Rhythm of reveals

Write out the sequence: what opens, when, and **how it is earned**. A reveal without the person's action is not a reveal; it is autoplay.

    bad     everything appears on its own, on a timer
    good    the person took a step — the world answered

## Don't

**Don't autoplay for longer than three seconds.** A person who waits leaves.

**Don't make people wait for beauty's sake.** A loading screen is a debt, not a genre.

**Don't change the rules silently.** If in one place scrolling moves the world and in another it scrolls text, that is not variety; it is breakage.
