# Stage 1. Scenario

What happens to the person who arrives, step by step.

## Gate

**Until `scenario.md` is written, stage 2 does not begin.**

## What goes into the scenario

### 1. Who arrives and why

Not "the user". A specific person with a specific reason. Usually two or three, and they want different things:

    recruiter      twenty seconds; wants to know "is it worth writing to them"
    colleague      came from a chat link; wants to see one thing
    the author     showing it to a friend on their own phone

The scenario is written for the main one. The others must not be obstructed.

Write down what the main visitor must know by the third second — usually whose work this is and what is here. The concept will decide *how* they learn it; it may never decide *whether* (hard rule 2).

> From the author's practice: a portfolio for a sound designer opened on a black field with one line of light and the words "stand still". No name, no list of work — the projects surfaced only if you held still. The concept was the strongest of its batch, and it lost a blind comparison to a plain page that simply showed the projects, each with its own small mechanic. The audio director it was written for would have closed the tab before holding still.

### 2. What they take away

One sentence the person will say about the work a day later, in their own words. If there is no such sentence, there is no scenario — only a set of sections.

### 3. The concept — in one sentence

**Come up with three, not one.** The first is always the dullest: it comes from what you already know how to do.

The test of a concept: **the sentence tells you what to do with the WHOLE thing.** Check it with four questions — navigation, headings, cursor, transitions. If the sentence says nothing about them, it is a trick, not a concept.

    trick      "shader deformation", "parallax on scroll", "planets in space"
    concept    "a portfolio you drive through in a car"
               "a stage where one light burns, and it goes out as the hero loses his mind"
               "an archive that fades while you read it"

Pick the one with more consequences, not the one that is easier to build.

Run the **punch check** (`check-punch.md`) on the chosen concept now.

### 4. The course — in steps, not sections

A section is a place. A step is an event: what the person saw, understood, and did next. Write events.

    0 s     what is visible BEFORE any action
    3 s     what stays in their head if they do nothing else
    ...     the first action they take themselves, and why
    ...     the moment everything exists for
    end     what they leave with

### 5. What is NOT included

A separate list. Especially when there is a lot of material: a scenario is above all a refusal. What we know but do not show, and why.

## When the material is a real person

Portfolio, personal site, a history of work. Then the scenario is written from **real data**, not generalities: repositories, dates, texts, mistakes, letters.

Two rules that must not be broken:

**Reading everything and publishing everything are different decisions.** Read the material in full to understand its shape. Only what the author has approved item by item goes on the page.

**Personal matters only with an explicit yes** (hard rule 9). List every personal item you intend to use and get a yes per item before writing the course.

## Don't

**Don't start from a trick** and build a site around it. The trick is good, the page around it turns out ordinary, and nobody is to blame.

**Don't take a concept that lives on one screen.** After the first screen the idea runs out and a feed of sections follows.

**Don't confuse a concept with a topic.** "A site about a coffee shop" is a topic. "A site that brews: the longer you look, the darker it gets" is a concept.
